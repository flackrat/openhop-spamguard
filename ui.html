                        How SpamGuard works

                        SpamGuard reads the channel messages your repeater hears and looks for the signs of a spammer: made-up names that change every message, lots of brand-new names arriving through one repeater, the same message sent under different names, and one name posting the same message over and over. Messages from this repeater's own companions are never blocked.

                        When it finds spam it adds a rule to openHop so your repeater stops passing it on. It never changes your own openHop rules, and every automatic block ends by itself once the spam stops.

                        openHop's packet list marks a dropped packet “Policy blocked packet (rule N)”. Each block on this page shows its openHop rule number, so you can see which block stopped it. In openHop's policy settings SpamGuard's rules are named after what they stop, with (yours) on blocks you added and (duplicate), (campaign), (repeating) or (auto) on ones SpamGuard made.

                        Spam, not opinions

                        SpamGuard stops how spam is sent (floods of made-up names, the same message under many names, one name repeating itself) and never what people say. Blocks on message text only stop names SpamGuard doesn't know yet, last at most a week, and need at least 12 characters of a message. People it knows are never blocked for a message; if one of them copies or repeats something, only the extra copies are held back for a while. You can't mark a known person's message as spam.

                        FloodGuard: floods of any kind

                        Some floods aren't channel messages at all: encrypted packets with a new identity on almost every one, sent over and over with long, padded routes, so a single message can arrive dozens of times. Nothing in them can be blocked by name or text. FloodGuard (on by default; it can be turned off on the FloodGuard page) watches something a sender can't fake instead: how much of the time this repeater's radio is busy receiving and sending packets. It first spends three days learning how busy this radio normally gets and how far packets normally travel, and only counts meanwhile; then it sets its own limits from that and checks them once a day. When flood traffic keeps the radio busier than its level for a minute, FloodGuard stops passing on flood packets that have already come further than the routes it normally hears, and tightens two hops at a time while the radio stays busy, never below the floor you set. It lifts by itself once the radio has been quiet for a while, and says so in Activity.

                        It never looks at who sent a packet or what it says, channel messages from people SpamGuard knows always get through, and direct (routed) messages, replies and acknowledgements are never touched, so the mesh keeps working for ordinary messages during a flood. Nearby repeaters each protect their own airtime, so a flood is held to the area it is actually swamping. The flood packets it stops are shown on the Overview graphs in their own colour, next to the spam, and How busy the radio was shows the busiest five minutes of each hour. While it's on, FloodGuard also keeps fixed hop limits for flood packets with no region (32 hops) and adverts (8 hops), the same as MeshCore's flood.max.unscoped and flood.max.advert. You can change both on its page, and 0 turns one off. In Monitor, FloodGuard only counts what it would have stopped.

                        Interference on the channel

                        openHop measures the noise on the radio channel every 30 seconds. FloodGuard learns what's normal for this site over the first six hours and, when the noise stays well above it for five minutes, says so on its page, on Health and in Activity, with how much of its normal hearing range the repeater still has. A nearby transmitter that never stops (faulty or non-compliant equipment) or jamming are the usual causes. Nothing can be blocked, but every episode is recorded with its times and levels, and Copy the record gives you a summary to send to whoever looks after the equipment or to your radio regulator.

                        Wardriving pings

                        Wardriving apps such as MeshMapper send a ping with the phone's GPS position on #wardriving and log every repeater that echoes it. On the Channels page you can stop this repeater echoing those pings, so it isn't put on those maps. Nothing else changes, and pings from this repeater's own companions still go out. It keeps the repeater off precise coverage maps, but its adverts (with a location, if you set one), its code in the routes of everything it repeats, and its radio signal itself still give it away to anyone determined to find it. Leaving the location out of adverts helps further.

                        Getting started

                        SpamGuard starts on Monitor. Messages it would have stopped are marked “Would be stopped”. If it is only catching spam, switch to Protect. If you don't, it switches itself once it has watched for at least a day and seen enough spam (or after three quiet days), and says so in Activity. The Overview shows when; press Stay in Monitor to keep it watching only. It won't switch by itself if you have marked anything as Not spam.

                        If you won't check in often, press Look after itself on the Overview. Updates, the protection level and route blocks then look after themselves, and a log of the last few days is kept on this device so the level can be worked out. Look at held messages now and then to free anyone genuine.

                        If it blocks someone genuine

                        Press Not spam on their message. Their name is trusted from then on, and if a text block caught them, that block is removed. If a whole repeater was blocked, remove that block above. Busy events and people repeating the same message are the usual causes. A gentler protection level makes both less likely.

                        Limits

                        SpamGuard only controls what your repeater forwards. People in direct range of the spammer will still hear him, so the more repeaters near the source run a filter, the better. It cannot read private channels or direct messages unless you give it the channel key. It never blocks a person for what they say: to hide someone you'd rather not read, use the block option in your own MeshCore app.

                        Starting again

                        During a big flood of made-up names through many repeaters at once, SpamGuard can start a 15-minute lockdown by itself: only people it knows get through, then it stops on its own. Newcomers are held for those minutes too; you can turn this off in Settings.

                        Start again from scratch (Advanced, Settings) clears every block, setting and exception, and the page then shows only what happens after the reset. Nothing recorded is thrown away: the evidence log keeps everything.

                        How FloodGuard works

                        Some floods aren't channel messages: encrypted packets with a new identity on almost every one, resent with long, padded routes until a message arrives dozens of times. Nothing in them can be blocked by name or text, and routes can be faked. What a sender can't fake is how busy this repeater's radio is, so FloodGuard measures that from every packet openHop hears and sends.

                        It starts by learning for three days how busy this radio normally gets and how far packets normally travel, counting only, then sets its own limits from that and checks them once a day; anything you set yourself is kept. When flood traffic keeps the radio busier than its level for a minute, FloodGuard stops passing on flood packets that have already come further than the routes it normally hears, tightening two hops at a time while the radio stays busy, never below the floor. It lifts by itself once the radio has been quiet for a while and notes each step in Activity. In Monitor it only counts.

                        It never looks at who sent a packet or what it says. Channel messages from people SpamGuard knows always get through, and direct (routed) messages, replies and acknowledgements are never touched, so ordinary messaging keeps working during a flood. Each repeater protects its own airtime, so a flood is held to the area it is swamping.
